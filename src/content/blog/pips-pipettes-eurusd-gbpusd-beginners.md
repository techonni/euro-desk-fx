A **pip** is a small, standard step in a currency pair’s price. On **EURUSD** and **GBPUSD**, beginners use pips to measure how far price moved, to size study notes, and to talk about risk in plain numbers — without guessing the next candle. A **pipette** (also called a fractional pip or a tenth of a pip) is an even smaller step many platforms show as an extra digit.

This guide stays in beginner English. You will learn what a pip is on EURUSD and GBPUSD, how pipettes fit in, how to count a move from one quote to another, how pip value links to lot size in soft study math, how sessions and spreads change the story, and calm notebook rules. This is education only. It is **not financial advice**. Nothing here is a trade order or a promise of profit.

## Why pips matter before fancy setups

If you already opened a chart, you saw prices like **1.08542** on EURUSD. Beginners often ask:

- How big was that move?
- Was my stop “tight” or “wide”?
- How do I compare a 20-pip swing on EURUSD to one on GBPUSD?

**Pips** give you a shared yardstick. Without them, every discussion stays vague (“it moved a bit”). With them, your journal can say “London open tagged +28 pips from the Asian low” — a sentence you can review later. Always **NFA**.

## Pip in plain words (EURUSD and GBPUSD)

For most **USD-quoted majors** like EURUSD and GBPUSD, one pip is a move of **0.0001** in the quote (the fourth decimal place when you ignore the tiny fifth digit).

Examples (teaching props only — not forecasts):

- EURUSD from **1.0850** to **1.0851** = **1 pip** higher
- EURUSD from **1.0850** to **1.0870** = **20 pips** higher
- GBPUSD from **1.2700** to **1.2685** = **15 pips** lower

Soft study language: “count the fourth decimal step.” Write the start price, the end price, then the pip distance.

**Soft study rule:** name the pair and the direction (up or down in pips) on every note. “Moved 20” without the pair trains noise, not skill.

### Tiny EURUSD example (illustrative only)

Asian session low near **1.0820**. London push reaches **1.0845**. Distance = **25 pips**. Your notebook can say: `EURUSD | London | +25 pips from Asian low | NFA`. Numbers are classroom props.

### Tiny GBPUSD example (illustrative only)

You mark a study level near **1.2750**. Price dips to **1.2720**. Distance = **30 pips**. Soft note: `GBPUSD | H1 | −30 pips from 1.2750 zone | spread noted | NFA`.

## What is a pipette?

Many platforms quote EURUSD and GBPUSD with **five decimals** (for example **1.08542**). The fifth digit is often called a **pipette** — one-tenth of a pip.

- Move from **1.08500** to **1.08501** = **1 pipette** (0.1 pip)
- Move from **1.08500** to **1.08510** = **1 pip** (10 pipettes)

Some brokers call this the “fractional pip” or “point.” Soft study tip: decide whether your journal counts **whole pips** or includes tenths. Mixing both mid-week makes reviews messy.

**Soft study rule:** pick one unit for the week (whole pips **or** pips+tenths) and stick to it on that notebook page.

## How to count pips step by step

Use this soft checklist on a demo chart or a replay note:

1. Write the **pair** (EURUSD or GBPUSD).
2. Write the **start quote** and the **end quote** (same session window if you can).
3. Subtract the smaller from the larger (or use absolute difference).
4. Divide by **0.0001** for whole pips on these pairs.
5. Round the way your rule says (nearest pip, or keep one decimal for pipettes).
6. Add direction: **+** if price rose, **−** if price fell (from your start to your end).
7. Tag **session**, **timeframe**, and **NFA**.

Example math (educational only):

- Start **1.09000**, end **1.09125**
- Difference **0.00125**
- ÷ **0.0001** = **12.5 pips** (or 125 pipettes)

You do not need a fancy calculator. Slow counting trains the eye.

## Pip value: why lot size shows up next

A pip is a **price step**. **Pip value** is roughly “how much account money that step is worth” for a given **position size**. Beginners meet this when they study lot size and stop distance together.

Soft classroom framing (not a live formula you must memorize day one):

- On EURUSD and GBPUSD, a **standard lot** (often labeled 1.0) is commonly taught as about **$10 per pip** when the account is in USD — **illustrative textbook talk**, not a promise for every broker or account currency.
- A **mini lot** (0.1) is often taught as about **$1 per pip**.
- A **micro lot** (0.01) is often taught as about **$0.10 per pip**.

Your broker’s contract size, account currency, and quote conventions can change the exact number. Soft study rule: read **your** platform’s pip-value display on a demo ticket before you trust a round “$10” story. Always **NFA**.

Link to other lessons you may already have:

- **Lot size / position sizing** — how many lots you study with
- **Stop loss** — how many pips of room you sketch for invalidation
- **Risk-reward** — reward distance in pips vs risk distance in pips

Pips are the shared unit across those notes.

## Spreads, costs, and “free” pips that are not free

The **spread** is the gap between bid and ask. Soft study: if the spread is **1.2 pips** on EURUSD in a quiet hour, a “+5 pip” study move may feel smaller after costs. On GBPUSD, spreads can widen faster around UK data.

Notebook line idea:

`EURUSD | H1 | move +18 pips | spread ~0.8 pip at entry note | NFA`

You are not “paying” a lecture fee. You are learning that **measured moves and tradable edges are different ideas**. Education only.

## Sessions change how big a “normal” pip swing looks

Match your classroom to the hour (Europe/Lisbon context is fine for labels):

- **Asian hours:** many EURUSD and GBPUSD days show smaller swings. A 15-pip range can be a full study sample.
- **London open:** ranges often expand. A 40-pip push is less “shocking” than the same print at 03:00.
- **London–New York overlap:** another active USD window; pip swings and spreads both deserve a note.
- **News windows:** spikes can print dozens of pips in minutes, then fade. Mark the calendar. Soft beginner approach: measure after spreads settle, or label the sample as news-fragile.

Soft study framing: do not copy a London “average pip day” onto a sleepy Asian sample without saying so.

## Soft study rules (not live trading rules)

Use these as classroom guardrails while you learn:

1. **Define the unit** — whole pips or pips+pipettes — before the session.
2. **Always name the pair** — EURUSD and GBPUSD are both “four decimal” majors, but they do not move identically.
3. **Write start and end quotes** — do not estimate “about twenty” from memory.
4. **Separate move size from setup quality** — a big pip swing is not automatically a good study trade.
5. **Note spread next to pip counts** when you review costs.
6. **One timeframe for the measurement** — do not mix M1 noise into an H1 pip count without labeling both.
7. **Always NFA** — yesterday’s 30-pip swing does not promise today’s.

These rules teach process. They do not tell you what to buy or sell.

## Common beginner mistakes

**1. Mixing pips and percent.**  
A 50-pip move is not “50%.” Keep units honest.

**2. Ignoring the fifth digit, then arguing about 0.3 pip.**  
Decide whether pipettes count this week.

**3. Comparing raw pip counts across pairs without context.**  
GBPUSD can be “noisier” than EURUSD in the same hour. Journal both.

**4. Treating pip value as universal.**  
Account currency and lot size change the money story. Check the platform.

**5. Calling every spike “huge” with no number.**  
Write the pip distance. Feelings are optional color.

**6. Measuring from a wick extreme then rewriting history.**  
Pick start/end rules (close-to-close, or extreme-to-extreme) and stick to them on that page.

**7. Skipping spread on GBPUSD news minutes.**  
Costs belong next to the pip count.

## How to practice pip counting in 15 minutes

No live money required.

1. Pick **EURUSD or GBPUSD** and one timeframe (H1 is fine).
2. Mark a **start** quote at the session open (or at a clear swing).
3. After 15–30 minutes of study time, write the **end** quote.
4. Count **pips** (and pipettes if that is your unit).
5. Note **spread** once.
6. Optional: sketch what a 15-pip and a 30-pip stop distance would look like on that chart — educational only, not a live order.
7. One sentence: was this hour “quiet,” “normal,” or “news-fragile” for pip size?

After ten notes, look for patterns:

- Does London print larger pip ranges than Asia on your sample?
- Is GBPUSD’s average study swing wider than EURUSD’s in the same window?
- Do news minutes inflate pip counts that later fade?

Patterns beat slogans.

## Quick checklist before you measure a move

Use this soft checklist on EURUSD and GBPUSD:

- [ ] Pair and timeframe are named  
- [ ] Start and end quotes are written  
- [ ] Unit is clear (pips vs pipettes)  
- [ ] Direction (+/−) is labeled  
- [ ] Session and any calendar event are noted  
- [ ] Spread is written at least once if you care about costs  
- [ ] You are not confusing pip size with “must trade now”  

If any box is empty, keep learning the measurement instead of forcing a trade story.

## Key takeaways

- On EURUSD and GBPUSD, one pip is typically a **0.0001** price step; a pipette is often a tenth of that.
- Count from written start and end quotes — do not guess.
- Pip value depends on lot size and account details; treat textbook “$10 per pip” as soft classroom talk until your platform confirms.
- Sessions, spreads, and news change how “normal” a pip swing looks.
- Soft study rules keep journals comparable across days.
- This is educational only — not financial advice.

Keep the language concrete: “EURUSD H1 +22 pips from 1.0840 to 1.0862” beats “it ran hard.” That clarity is a beginner superpower.
